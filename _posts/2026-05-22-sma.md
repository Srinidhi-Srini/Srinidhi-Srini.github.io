## Introduction
Shape Memory Alloys (SMAs) are a type of smart materials capable of "remembering" its original shape and returning to it after deformation caused by external stimuli. This is known as the shape memory effect and it comes from the reversible phase transformation between two crystal structures: martensite and austenite. Some SMAs also have superelasticity, which is a characteristic of a material that can undergo large strains without permanent deformation. This material’s properties make it a good candidate for making a variety of intricate devices like stents and wires compared to conventional materials like stainless steel. To understand SMAs better, one must learn about their history, the science behind them and current applications. 

## History and Development of Shape Memory Alloys
One of the most widely studied SMAs is NiTi, or Nitinol, a 1:1 alloy of nickel and titanium that was discovered in 1959 by William J. Buehler at the Naval Ordnance Laboratory. Buehler was tasked with making a missile nose cone that could survive reentry through the Earth’s atmosphere. He experimented with ingots of various alloys by drop testing them after cooling from high temperatures. He noticed that the cooled and uncooled nitinol nuggets made different sounds, and discovered that this was due to the change in the material’s crystal structure during the cooling process. During a 1961 presentation, a senior official repeatedly folded Nitinol like an accordion and noticed that heating it reversed the folding. This would later be known as the shape memory effect. By the late 1970s, Nitinol was beginning to be used for medical applications due to its superelastic property. Stents, devices that keep blood vessels open after procedures, were one of the first medical devices to be made using SMAs. These stents could be compressed so flat that they could be pushed through a patient’s bloodstream, and once there, they could endure severe deformation. Over the next few decades in the late 1900s and early 2000s, improvements were made with surface treatment and design optimization that led to more widespread use of Nitinol in stents and other devices like wires (Baron).

## The Science Behind the Shape Memory Effect
SMAs like Nitinol are able to “remember” their original shape due to reversible, solid-state phase transformations. Nitinol can exist in two different phases. At high temperatures, the material is in the austenite phase, which has a highly symmetric cubic crystal structure. When the material is cooled or stressed, it then transforms into the martensite phase, which has a lower symmetric monoclinic structure. Due to the low symmetry, it can undergo large deformations through a process called twinning where the crystal structure is rearranged without breaking any of the bonds. When a deformed martensitic SMA is heated above the austenite finish temperature (AF), the material returns back to austenite. Since there’s only one stable configuration, it becomes the original shape (Wadood). The AF is also usually around the body temperature, which is convenient for medical devices. Additionally, when the material is heated, it stretches. But, what sets it apart from other materials is that this stretching seems to deform it, but it just snaps back to the original shape when the stressor is removed. The deformation temperatures can also be altered through specific composition and heat treatments (University of Washington).

## Medical Applications
The reversible deformation and superelastic properties of Nitinol make it a commonly used material for many medical devices. One is orthodontic wires. Orthodontists use arched nitinol wires because they apply a constant force on the teeth since the wire tries to return back to its original straight shape (Stanford Advanced Materials).

Stents are another common application, used in cardiovascular issues or aneurysms to keep the blood vessels open. The paper “An overview of nitinol medical applications” by T. Duerig, A. Pelton and D. Stockel gives 11 reasons why Nitinol is perfect for stents. To briefly summarize, nitinol can first efficiently deploy a medical device using the shape memory effect as it can be compressed to fit through small openings and then the body temperature makes it revert to its desired shape. It is also biocompatible and is accepted by the human body, is MRI compatible due to lower magnetic susceptibility, can endure constant stress over a long period of time and is fatigue resistant (Duerig et al.). Compared to stainless steel, nitinol is “capable of undergoing a 30% deformation with a cycles to failure life expectancy greater than 10,000,000” while stainless steel on the other hand “could withstand a deformation of just 0.5% with fatigue life of around 1 million cycles” (Baron).



## Conclusion
Shape Memory Alloys like Nitinol have revolutionized modern medical devices due to their unique ability to recover their original shape after deformation. The phase transformations between martensite and austenite produce this property and allow for countless applications that would be impossible with conventional materials. From cardiovascular stents that save lives to orthodontic wires that straighten teeth, they have made a major impact in our daily lives. With reversible deformation, fatigue resistance and excellent biocompatibility, SMAs like Nitinol improve recovery while being minimally invasive. Research continues to take place that makes producing and using these materials more efficient, giving way to innovative medical solutions and showing how materials science plays a direct role in improving our lives and health.
